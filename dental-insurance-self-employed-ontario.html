Dental insurance for the self-employed in Ontario

OHIP does not cover routine dental care for most adults. No cleanings, no fillings, no crowns. With an employer you would not notice; self-employed, every bill is yours. A self employed dental plan fixes that — but the number that decides whether it was worth buying is not the premium. It is the annual maximum, and on individual plans that figure is smaller than people expect.

What OHIP leaves you with

Ontario's health plan covers physicians and hospitals. For a working-age adult it does not cover routine dentistry at all. The province runs targeted programs — dental surgery performed in hospital, children in lower-income households, some seniors — but none of those is the self-employed thirty-, forty- or fifty-something paying for a cleaning twice a year.

Two cleanings, an exam and x-rays is a few hundred dollars a year before anything goes wrong. One crown or root canal is a four-figure decision. That is the gap, and it is permanent: unlike the OHIP waiting period for newcomers, it does not resolve with time.

How a dental plan is actually built

Individual plans split dental into tiers and reimburse each at a different rate. The shape is consistent across insurers even when the numbers differ:

TierWhat is in itHow it behaves
Preventive & diagnosticCleanings, exams, x-rays, fluorideReimbursed at the highest percentage. Often available immediately or after a short wait.
Basic restorativeFillings, extractions, some periodontal workMiddle percentage. Commonly a waiting period of a few months.
MajorCrowns, bridges, dentures, some root canalsLowest percentage, longest waiting period — often a year or more — and excluded from entry-level plans entirely.

Orthodontics, where offered at all, is normally a separate option with its own maximum.

The mistake almost everyone makes: comparing the monthly premium. Two plans at the same price can differ by a factor of three on what they will actually pay out, because the annual maximum and the major-work percentage are where the real difference sits. Compare those first and the premium last.

The annual maximum is the whole game

The annual maximum is the most the plan reimburses for dental in a policy year. On an employer plan it is usually generous enough that nobody thinks about it. On an individual plan it is modest, and it is the figure that decides whether buying the plan made you money or cost you money.

Work it out the honest way, with your own history:

  1. What did you actually spend on dental in each of the last three years?
  2. What has your dentist already told you is coming — a crown, a replacement filling, periodontal work?
  3. What would the plan have reimbursed on that history, after the percentages and the annual maximum?

If the answer is "about what I would have paid in premiums", the plan is not insurance, it is prepayment with extra steps. If your dentist has flagged major work, the answer usually changes sharply — but see the waiting periods first, because that is where the timing catches people.

Waiting periods, and why applying early is cheaper

You generally cannot buy a plan the week a crown gets quoted and claim on it. Preventive work often starts quickly; basic restorative typically waits a few months; major work frequently waits a year or more.

Most individual health and dental plans are also medically underwritten — there are health questions, and existing conditions or work your dentist has already recommended can be excluded or priced for. Some plans are guaranteed issue with no questions at all; those accept everyone, and pay for it with lower maximums, longer waits and a higher premium for the coverage you get.

Both facts point the same way: apply while nothing is wrong. The cheapest version of this decision is the one made a year before you need it, which is also the one nobody makes.

If your business is incorporated, your options are different

This is the part most comparison articles get wrong, and the distinction that matters is incorporated company versus sole proprietorship — not "self-employed" as one category.

An incorporated business may be able to fund medical and dental costs through the corporation using a health spending arrangement rather than a conventional insurance plan. That changes how the cost is treated and, for some owners, changes the arithmetic entirely. The rules differ by business structure, and the published guidance on the details is genuinely inconsistent — including on whether an unincorporated sole proprietor qualifies at all, and on what limits apply.

So this page will not quote you figures. Plenty of articles state specific annual caps with great confidence; I could not verify those against the Canada Revenue Agency, and a number that is nearly right is worse than no number when it is a tax position. The tax treatment is a question for your accountant. Cover & Protect is a licensed insurance advisory practice and does not give tax advice. What we can do is price the insurance side properly against whichever route your accountant says fits your structure.

If you employ anyone, a small group benefits plan becomes available as a third option, and it usually buys more coverage per dollar than individual plans do.

Before you compare plans

  1. Pull your last three years of dental spending.
  2. Ask your dentist what is coming in the next two years, in writing if they will.
  3. Decide whether you also need prescription drug and paramedical cover — most plans bundle dental with health, and buying dental alone is not always possible.
  4. If incorporated, ask your accountant about the health spending route before you buy an individual plan.
  5. Then compare: annual maximum, reimbursement percentage by tier, waiting periods, and whether underwriting will exclude anything. Premium last.

Frequently asked questions

Does OHIP cover dental care for adults in Ontario?

Not routine dental care, for most adults. OHIP covers certain dental surgery performed in hospital, and the province runs targeted programs for children in lower-income households and for some seniors, but cleanings, fillings, crowns and root canals are not covered for a working-age adult. Without an employer plan the bill is yours.

What does self employed dental insurance actually cover?

Individual plans split dental into three tiers reimbursed at different rates. Preventive and diagnostic work — cleanings, exams, x-rays — is reimbursed at the highest percentage. Basic restorative work such as fillings and extractions sits in the middle. Major work such as crowns, bridges and dentures is reimbursed at the lowest percentage, often has a waiting period, and is sometimes excluded from entry-level plans entirely.

What is the annual maximum and why does it matter more than the premium?

It is the most the plan will reimburse for dental in a policy year, and on individual plans it is modest compared with an employer plan. It decides whether the plan was worth buying, because a single crown or root canal can consume most of a year's maximum on an entry-level plan. Compare the maximum and the reimbursement percentages first, the premium second.

Is there a waiting period for dental insurance?

Usually, and it depends on the tier. Preventive work often starts immediately or after a short wait. Basic restorative commonly waits a few months. Major work frequently waits a year or more. Buying a plan the week before a crown is quoted generally does not work.

Can I get dental insurance if I have existing dental problems?

Often yes, but the terms change. Most individual plans are medically underwritten, so existing conditions and work already recommended by a dentist may be excluded or priced for. Some plans are guaranteed issue with no health questions; those accept everyone but carry lower maximums, longer waiting periods and a higher premium for the coverage.

Is dental insurance different if my business is incorporated?

The options are genuinely different, and incorporated versus sole proprietor matters more than people expect. An incorporated business may be able to fund medical and dental costs through the corporation using a health spending arrangement rather than a conventional plan. The rules differ by structure and published guidance is inconsistent, so the tax treatment is a question for your accountant and the coverage is one for a licensed advisor.
