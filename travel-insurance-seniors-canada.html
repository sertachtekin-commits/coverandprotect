Travel insurance for seniors: what changes at 60, 70 and 75

Canadian seniors can buy emergency medical travel insurance, and a pre-existing condition is not by itself a refusal. But from about 60, three things change on the application: a medical questionnaire becomes mandatory, the stability period that governs pre-existing conditions gets longer, and the premium moves in age bands that step up sharply after 75. Those three things, not your destination, decide both what you pay and whether a claim is paid.

The three things that change with age

A 35-year-old buying a week in Florida answers almost nothing and pays almost nothing. The same trip at 72 is a different transaction, and it is worth knowing exactly how before you start filling in forms.

What changesWhat it means for you
Medical questionnaireBecomes mandatory at a certain age, often around 60. It sets your rate band. It does not decide what is covered, and answering it inaccurately can trigger an extra deductible or a declined claim.
Stability periodThe window before departure in which a pre-existing condition must not have changed. Commonly 90 or 180 days, and it generally lengthens with age. This is the clause that decides claims.
Age bandsPremiums do not rise smoothly; they step at band boundaries. The steepest steps are after 75. Buying before a birthday that crosses a band can lock in the lower rate.

The practical point: the windows, the questionnaire and the age bands are each insurer's own wording, not an industry standard. That is precisely why comparing matters more at 70 than it did at 40 — the same medical history can sit inside one insurer's window and outside another's.

The stability period is the one that costs people money

Most seniors who lose a large claim did not buy the wrong plan. They bought a perfectly good plan and were not stable through the window, usually without knowing that anything had happened.

A condition stops being stable if, inside the window before you leave, any of the following happened:

  • a new diagnosis, or a new or returning symptom
  • a hospital admission, or a visit to a specialist for the condition
  • a test, investigation or referral that has not yet produced a result
  • a change in medication — a new drug, a stopped drug, or a dose going up or down

The medication trap. A blood pressure dose adjusted at a routine appointment six weeks before departure is a change to the condition at most insurers. The policy is still valid; the heart-related claim is the part at risk. A switch to an equivalent generic of the same drug at the same dose is normally not a change — but the definitions differ, so this is the one detail worth checking with an advisor rather than guessing.

Two things follow from that. First, "stable" is not a feeling — you can feel completely well and still fail the test. Second, if something did change inside the window, that is a reason to compare insurers, not a reason to assume you cannot travel. Windows are set by each insurer's own wording, so a history that misses one may sit comfortably inside another's.

The detail, including a printable pre-departure checklist, is in the stability period guide.

The questionnaire sets the price, not the coverage

These two get confused constantly, and the confusion is expensive. The medical questionnaire is what the insurer asks you at the quote stage, and its job is to put you in a rate band. The policy wording is what a claim is actually assessed against. Passing the questionnaire does not mean a condition is covered.

The questions are more specific than people expect, and they are worth reading slowly:

  • A cardiac event is often asked as a recency band — less than a year, one to five years, more than five — rather than as a date. Which band "about a year ago" falls into is what moves the rate.
  • Blood pressure is often asked on its own, pivoting on whether medication was altered within a set number of months. Long-term patients routinely answer this wrong in both directions.
  • A condition controlled by medication, a device, diet or exercise still counts as a yes. "It's managed, so I don't have it" is the most common honest mistake on these forms.
  • Every question must be answered, including choosing None where nothing applies. Insurers state that an inaccurate or incomplete questionnaire can trigger an additional deductible, which at 60 and over can run to tens of thousands of dollars on a claim.

What it costs

Age is the dominant factor and the curve steepens after 75. On one published 2026 long-stay rate card the daily rate was about $8.47 at ages 65 to 69, rising to about $29.36 at ages 80 to 84 — more than triple for the same coverage. Trip length, destination, deductible and declared conditions move it from there.

Two levers are worth knowing about before you shop:

  • The deductible. Taking a deductible lowers the premium, sometimes substantially. Whether that is a good trade depends on what you would comfortably pay out of pocket on a bad day.
  • Age band timing. If a birthday before departure moves you into the next band, buying beforehand can hold the lower rate for the whole policy.

Trip length matters more than most people think

An annual multi-trip plan looks like the obvious choice for someone who travels several times a year, and for many seniors it is. But the number that matters is your longest single trip, not how many trips you take.

Annual plans cover unlimited trips and cap each one at a set number of days — commonly 9, 17, 30 or 60. The clock runs from the day you leave Canada to the day you return. A 150-day winter on a 35-day cap means you are covered for the first 35 days and uninsured for the remaining 115, which is not a gap anyone notices until a hospital asks for a policy number.

  • Several trips a year, none longer than the cap → compare annual multi-trip plans.
  • One long winter away → that is a long-stay policy, not an annual plan. See snowbird travel insurance.
  • Already away and need to extend → there are rules that catch people out. See top-ups and extensions.

Your provincial health plan is not travel coverage — and you need to keep it

A provincial plan such as OHIP pays a small fraction of what a hospital outside Canada charges. On a serious admission the uncovered balance runs into six figures, which is the whole reason travel medical insurance exists.

There is a second, less obvious reason to pay attention to it. Insurers generally define an eligible Canadian resident as someone who is enrolled in, or eligible for, a provincial or territorial health plan. If that provincial coverage lapses, the travel policy can go with it — which is the reverse of what most people assume, and it is the trap for anyone spending long stretches outside the province. Ontario's day-count rules are set out in the OHIP 212-day rule guide.

Before you buy: a short checklist

  1. Write down your longest single trip in days, Canada to Canada.
  2. List every medication, with the date of the last change to any dose.
  3. Note anything outstanding — a test awaiting results, a referral, a symptom being investigated. These need a conversation before a purchase, not after.
  4. Check whether a birthday falls before departure and crosses an age band.
  5. Confirm your provincial health coverage is current and will remain so.
  6. Then compare — the premium is identical whether you buy through an advisor or direct, so advice costs nothing.

Frequently asked questions

Can seniors still get travel insurance in Canada?

Yes. Canadian insurers write emergency medical travel insurance well into the eighties, and a pre-existing condition is not by itself a refusal. What changes with age is the paperwork and the wording: from about 60 a medical questionnaire becomes mandatory, the stability period gets longer, and the premium moves in age bands that step up sharply after 75. Maximum ages and the exact windows differ by insurer, which is why comparing matters more at 70 than at 40.

What is a stability period and why does it matter for seniors?

It is the stretch of time immediately before departure during which a pre-existing condition must not have changed, for that condition to be covered. Commonly 90 or 180 days, and it usually lengthens with age. A change means a new diagnosis, a new symptom, a hospital admission, a test still outstanding, or a change in medication including a dose going up or down. If a condition was not stable through the whole window, treatment related to it can be excluded even though the policy was bought and paid for.

Does a change in my medication affect my travel insurance?

It can, and it is the detail most travellers miss. A dose increase or decrease, stopping a medication, or starting a new one is generally treated as a change to the condition, which restarts the stability clock. A switch to an equivalent generic of the same drug at the same dose is normally not a change. The definitions differ between insurers, so a medication adjustment in the weeks before departure is worth a conversation before you buy, not after.

What does the medical questionnaire actually do?

It sets the rate band for your quote. It does not decide whether a condition is covered — the policy wording does that. The questions are specific in ways that catch people out: a cardiac event asked as a recency band rather than a date, a pivot on whether medication changed inside a set number of months, and a condition controlled by medication, a device, diet or exercise still counting as a yes. Insurers state that an inaccurate or incomplete answer can trigger an additional deductible or a declined claim.

How much does travel insurance cost for a senior?

Age is the biggest driver and the curve steepens after 75. On one published 2026 long-stay rate card the daily rate was about $8.47 at ages 65 to 69 and about $29.36 at ages 80 to 84 — more than triple for the same coverage. Trip length, destination, deductible and declared conditions move it from there. Those figures are illustrative; the insurer sets the actual premium from your completed application.

Is an annual multi-trip plan a good idea for a senior?

It depends on your longest single trip, not on how many trips you take. An annual plan covers unlimited trips but caps each one at a set number of days, commonly 9, 17, 30 or 60, measured from the day you leave Canada to the day you return. A 150-day winter on a 35-day cap leaves you covered for 35 days and uninsured for the rest. Count your longest trip first, then compare.

Does my provincial health plan cover me outside Canada?

Barely. A provincial plan such as OHIP pays a small fraction of what a hospital outside Canada charges, and the gap on a serious admission runs into six figures. Keeping provincial coverage also matters for a second reason: insurers generally define an eligible Canadian resident as someone enrolled in, or eligible for, a provincial or territorial health plan. If that coverage lapses, the travel policy can go with it.
