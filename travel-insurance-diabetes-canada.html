Travel insurance with diabetes: every scenario

Having diabetes is not a refusal. It is one of the most common conditions Canadian travel insurers see, and it is written every day — diet-controlled, three tablets, insulin, type 1. What decides your case is not the diagnosis. It is whether the condition has been stable through the window your policy sets before you leave. This page walks every case from a borderline reading to type 1 on insulin, and explains the dose-adjustment rule that most diabetic travellers get wrong in their own disfavour.

The one thing that decides it

Insurers do not ask "do you have diabetes" and then refuse you. They ask whether anything about it changed in the period before you travel. That period is the stability window, and it is commonly 90, 180 or 365 days depending on the insurer, the plan and your age. Older travellers generally face longer windows.

So the real question is not how serious your diabetes is. It is how long has it looked exactly like this? A type 1 diabetic on insulin for twenty years with nothing changed is often in a simpler position than someone with mild type 2 whose tablet was switched last month.

Say this out loud once and the rest of the page makes sense: stable serious diabetes is usually easier to insure than unstable mild diabetes.

Find your scenario

Every stage, from a borderline blood test to type 1. Find the rung you are on.

1 · Pre-diabetes or a borderline reading

Declare it anyway. Questionnaires generally ask about conditions you have had, whether or not you were treated, which captures a borderline result sitting in your file. Declaring costs you very little — no medication and no treatment change rarely moves anything. Not declaring is what creates the problem, because the insurer reads your medical records when you claim, not when you buy.

2 · Controlled by diet and exercise only

This is where the most common honest mistake happens. People answer "no" because they take nothing. But a condition controlled by diet, exercise, a device or medication is still a pre-existing condition, and most forms say so in the question itself. Declare it. Diet-controlled diabetes with nothing changed is usually about as straightforward as a pre-existing condition gets.

3 · One oral medication

Routine, provided the dose has been steady. The question that matters is when did anything about it last change — and see the dose-adjustment section below, because the answer is friendlier than most people assume.

4 · Two or three oral medications

Still routine. Two things to know. First, several insurers ask about the total number of medications you take across all conditions, not just diabetes, and five or more is a common point at which the questions get closer. Second, more medications means more opportunities for one of them to have changed recently — and it only takes one.

5 · Insulin, type 2

Insulin is the dividing line on most forms. It does not mean refusal; it means a different answer on the questionnaire and often a different rate band. If you have been on insulin for years at a steady dose, you are in a good position. If you started insulin recently, that is a treatment change and the clock restarts from that date — read the next section before you conclude anything from that.

6 · Type 1 diabetes

Lifelong, insulin-dependent, and frequently very stable — which is exactly what the wording rewards. Type 1 travellers are often surprised to find their case is cleaner than a recently diagnosed type 2's, because nothing has changed in years. The form will ask about insulin, not about type.

The dose adjustment rule — read this one twice

Diabetics adjust doses. That is what managing diabetes is. And most diabetic travellers assume any adjustment has broken their stability window, so they either give up or buy the wrong plan quietly.

Many Canadian policies specifically carve out routine dose adjustments within prescribed parameters for insulin or oral diabetes medication, where your levels are being checked regularly and the condition itself is unchanged. It is written into the definition of what counts as a change. If that is what happened to you, your stability period may never have been broken at all.

Other carve-outs commonly appear alongside it: switching to an equivalent generic or brand at the same dose, and several medications being combined into one where the condition is unchanged.

When diabetes gets more complicated

Complications are treated as conditions in their own right, not as part of the diabetes answer, and each carries its own stability requirement. Declare them separately:

  • Neuropathy, retinopathy or diabetic foot problems
  • Kidney disease — some questionnaires ask about kidney disease requiring dialysis as its own separate question
  • Cardiac or circulatory conditions, which frequently travel with long-standing diabetes and are usually asked as a recency band rather than a date

The hard stops. Some situations stop a sale rather than price it, and no amount of comparing changes them: a test result you are still waiting for, a symptom under investigation, a referral not yet seen, a hospital admission in the last few months, or travelling in order to get treatment. An unresolved investigation is generally excluded along with anything connected to it. If any of those apply, talk to an advisor before you buy — that is the whole point of calling one.

Snowbirds with diabetes: four extra things

A fortnight in Portugal and a five-month winter in Florida are different products. If you are going south for the season, these catch people out far more often than the diabetes question does:

WhatWhy it matters with diabetes
The per-trip day capAn annual multi-trip plan covers unlimited trips but caps each one — commonly 9, 17, 30 or 60 days, measured Canada to Canada. A 150-day winter on a 35-day cap means 35 days covered and 115 uninsured, with a chronic condition, a long way from home.
Coming home at ChristmasUnder some wordings, returning to Canada terminates the policy, with only an unexpected visit exempted. Plan the trip home before you buy, not after.
Losing provincial coverageInsurers generally define an eligible Canadian resident as someone enrolled in or eligible for a provincial health plan. Stay away too long and losing OHIP can end the travel policy with it — the reverse of what most people assume.
Insulin supply and storageNot an insurance question, but a real one: supply for the whole season, cooling in transit, and a plan if a vial is lost. Your pharmacist, not your insurer, is the person to ask.

The day-count rules for Ontario are in the 212-day OHIP rule guide, and the long-stay options are in snowbird travel insurance.

Visitors to Canada and Super Visa applicants with diabetes

The principle is the same: stability wording applies to pre-existing conditions, and stable diabetes is routinely written on visitor and Super Visa policies. Two differences worth knowing.

Super Visa adds a requirement that is not about health at all — the policy must provide at least $100,000 of emergency medical cover, valid for one year, as a condition of the visa itself. And for a parent or grandparent arriving with diabetes and any recent treatment change, the comparison between insurers matters far more than the premium does, because the wording is where the difference sits. Answering the medical questionnaire

The questionnaire sets your rate band. It does not decide what is covered — the policy wording does that. Four things diabetic travellers routinely get wrong:

  1. A controlled condition is still a yes. Diet, exercise, a device or medication — all still count.
  2. Answer every question, including "None" where nothing applies. A blank is not an answer.
  3. Know your dates. "Sometime in the spring" is not good enough. Find the date of the last change to any medication before you start.
  4. Do not round in your own favour. Insurers state that an inaccurate or incomplete questionnaire can trigger an additional deductible or a declined claim — and at 60 and over that extra deductible can run to tens of thousands of dollars, applied at the exact moment the bill is largest.

Your five-minute checklist

  1. Write down the date of the last change to every diabetes medication, including insulin.
  2. For each change, note why: routine adjustment, or because control worsened? That one word decides which column you are in.
  3. List every other medication you take, for anything.
  4. Note any complication — eyes, feet, kidneys, nerves, heart — and when it was diagnosed.
  5. Note anything outstanding: a test awaiting results, a referral, a symptom being looked at.
  6. Write down your longest single trip in days, Canada to Canada.
  7. Then compare — the premium is identical whether you buy through an advisor or direct, so the advice costs nothing.

Frequently asked questions

Can you get travel insurance if you have diabetes?

Yes. Diabetes is one of the most common conditions Canadian travel insurers see, and having it is not a refusal at any stage from pre-diabetes to type 1 on insulin. What decides the outcome is not the diagnosis but whether the condition has been stable through the window the policy sets before departure — commonly 90, 180 or 365 days depending on the insurer, the plan and your age.

Does a change in my insulin or diabetes medication dose break my travel insurance?

Often not, and this is the most misunderstood point for diabetic travellers. Many Canadian policies specifically carve out routine dose adjustments within prescribed parameters for insulin or oral diabetes medication, where levels are checked regularly and the condition itself is unchanged. Starting a new drug, stopping one, or a change because control worsened is different and generally does count. The wording is each insurer's own, so confirm it in your policy rather than assuming.

Does it matter whether I have type 1 or type 2 diabetes?

Less than most people expect. Insurer questionnaires typically do not ask which type you have — they split diabetes on whether it requires insulin or does not. A type 2 diabetic on insulin and a type 1 diabetic are often answering the same question. What moves the assessment is insulin use, how long things have been stable, the number of medications, and whether complications have developed.

I control my diabetes with diet and exercise only. Do I still have to declare it?

Yes. A condition controlled by diet, exercise, a medical device or medication still counts as a pre-existing condition, and questionnaires usually say so explicitly. Answering no because it is well managed is one of the most common honest mistakes on these forms, and an inaccurate answer can mean a declined claim or an additional deductible.

Is pre-diabetes a pre-existing condition for travel insurance?

Treat it as one and declare it. Questionnaires generally ask about conditions you have had whether or not you received care, which captures a borderline result. Declaring costs little, since a pre-diabetic reading with no medication and no treatment change rarely changes much. Not declaring is what creates a problem, because the insurer reads your records at claim time, not at purchase.

Does taking two or three diabetes medications make a difference?

The number itself can matter — several insurers ask about the total number of medications you take, not only about the condition, and five or more across all conditions is a common threshold for closer questions. For diabetes specifically, what matters more is when any of them last changed. Three long-stable medications is usually simpler than one adjusted six weeks ago because control had slipped.

I started insulin recently. Can I still travel?

Starting insulin is a treatment change rather than a routine dose adjustment, so it generally restarts the stability clock from that date. That does not end the conversation. Stability windows are each insurer's own wording rather than an industry standard, so a date outside one insurer's window can sit inside another's, and some insurers offer a paid add-on that buys a shorter window. Speak to a licensed advisor before assuming the answer is no.

What about complications such as neuropathy, retinopathy or kidney problems?

These change the picture and should be declared separately rather than folded into the diabetes answer. A complication is usually treated as its own condition with its own stability requirement, and some questionnaires ask about kidney disease requiring dialysis as a distinct question. Anything currently being investigated, any outstanding test result, or any referral not yet seen is more serious again.

I am a snowbird going south for the winter with diabetes. What should I watch?

Four things beyond stability. The per-trip day cap on annual plans, measured Canada to Canada, which can leave most of a long winter uninsured. Coming home mid-winter, which can terminate the policy under some wordings. Provincial health coverage, because losing it can end the travel policy with it. And insulin supply and storage for a whole season, which is a question for your pharmacist rather than your insurer.

Will having diabetes make my travel insurance more expensive?

It can, because the questionnaire sets a rate band and a declared condition may move you into a higher one. It does not automatically. Age, trip length, destination and deductible usually move the premium more than a stable, well-controlled diabetes answer does. What costs far more is answering inaccurately to get a lower band.

Do visitors to Canada and Super Visa applicants with diabetes get covered?

Often yes, on the same principle — stability wording applies much as it does to outbound travel policies, and stable diabetes is routinely written. Super Visa adds a separate requirement: at least $100,000 of emergency medical cover valid for one year, as a condition of the visa itself.
