Travel insurance with a heart condition

Cardiac history is the first thing every medical questionnaire asks about, and the one people answer worst. The good news first: very few heart conditions are refused by name. What makes a case difficult is almost never the diagnosis — it is how recently something happened, whether anything has changed since, and whether anything is still unresolved. This page covers stents, bypass, heart attack, angina, AFib and heart failure, and answers the blood thinner question that nothing online answers properly.

First: the blood thinner question

This is the most common point of confusion in the whole subject, and the reason is that insurers genuinely disagree — so people who ask around get different answers and conclude nobody knows.

Here is the structure. A blood thinner is a medication, not a condition. Some Canadian questionnaires name warfarin or Coumadin outright in their medication question. Others never mention anticoagulants at all, and the information reaches the insurer only through the question about your heart or circulatory condition. Neither approach is assessing the drug. Both are assessing why you are on it.

The trap that catches honest people. Questionnaires written years ago often name warfarin or Coumadin specifically. Someone on a newer anticoagulant — apixaban, rivaroxaban and the like — reads that list, does not see their drug, and answers no in perfectly good faith. The question is almost always meant to capture any blood thinner. If the form names only warfarin and you take something else, declare it anyway and put it in writing. An answer that is technically defensible but misleading is still what a claims assessor will find.

So what do you actually declare?

Work backwards from the reason you were prescribed it. The thinner comes along for the ride; the condition is the declarable thing.

If the reason is an irregular heartbeat

This is the one worth slowing down for, because it is the most common reason an older Canadian is on a blood thinner — and the one people are least likely to describe as a heart condition. Many say "I just take something to thin my blood." They do not think of themselves as a cardiac patient at all.

Here is why the prescription exists. In atrial fibrillation the upper chambers of the heart quiver rather than beat cleanly, so blood no longer moves through them properly. It pools — mostly in a small pouch called the left atrial appendage, where over 90% of AFib-related clots form — and a clot that breaks loose travels to the brain. The blood thinner is there to stop that happening. Doctors weigh it using a stroke-risk score alongside bleeding risk, which is why two people with the same rhythm can be on different treatment.

Now connect that to the form. The entire purpose of the medication is preventing a stroke — and stroke is the single most-asked-about event on a travel insurance questionnaire, sitting in the same recency band as a heart attack. So a blood thinner taken for AFib is not an incidental pill. It is treatment for a named heart condition whose whole significance is the event insurers ask about first.

What that means practically, if AFib is your reason:

  • Declare the AFib itself. It is named as a heart condition on several Canadian questionnaires, and some ask about it separately from other arrhythmias. Declaring the thinner but not the rhythm is an incomplete answer, and so is the reverse.
  • Declare the medication too — the anticoagulant, and any rate or rhythm drug such as a beta blocker or amiodarone, with the date each last changed.
  • If you have had a stroke or TIA because of it, that is a third declaration, answered by recency band under the events question. One situation, three things to disclose.
  • AFib is usually long-term, and for the questionnaire that works in your favour. Years of the same rhythm on unchanged medication is a stable, straightforward case. It is the recent change that complicates things.

What counts as a change with AFib. A new rate or rhythm medication, starting anticoagulation for the first time, or a dose changed because control slipped — those generally restart the clock. A cardioversion or an ablation is a procedure, so a recent one restarts it and a scheduled one you have not yet had is a hard stop at most insurers, along with anything related to it. Routine warfarin tuning against your INR, on the other hand, is usually the carve-out described above rather than a change at all.

And the part that works in your favour. Policy wording commonly carves out routine dose adjustments to blood thinners within prescribed parameters, where blood levels are checked regularly and the underlying condition is unchanged. Some wording also allows stopping a blood thinner for up to 24 hours for a surgery or procedure without that counting as a change. Anyone on warfarin has their dose tuned constantly — so this carve-out matters enormously, and most people never learn it exists. Confirm it in your own policy; definitions differ.

What "severe" actually means to an insurer

People assume there is a list of heart conditions that get refused. There mostly is not. Severity, in the sense the form cares about, is built from three things — and the diagnosis is not one of them.

The genuine hard stops. These end a sale rather than price it, at most insurers, and no amount of comparing changes them: a test result you are still waiting for, a symptom under investigation, a referral you have not yet attended, a hospital admission in the last few months, or travelling in order to get treatment. An unresolved investigation is generally excluded along with anything connected to it — and with a cardiac history, "anything connected to it" is a wide net. If any of these apply, speak to an advisor before you buy, not after.

Everything else is a conversation about which insurer's wording fits your dates. Stability windows are set by each insurer, not by an industry rule — so a history that falls outside one company's window can sit comfortably inside another's, and some insurers sell a paid add-on that buys a shorter window. Missing one insurer by a few weeks is a reason to compare, not a reason to stop.

Snowbirds with a cardiac history

A winter in Florida is a different product from a fortnight in Lisbon, and with a heart condition the differences get expensive:

  • The per-trip day cap. An annual multi-trip plan covers unlimited trips but caps each one, commonly at 9, 17, 30 or 60 days, measured from leaving Canada to returning. A 150-day winter on a 35-day cap means 115 days uninsured with a cardiac history, a long way from your cardiologist.
  • Going home at Christmas. Under some wordings, returning to Canada terminates the policy, with only an unexpected visit exempted. Plan the trip home before you buy.
  • Losing provincial coverage. Insurers generally define an eligible Canadian resident as someone enrolled in or eligible for a provincial health plan. Stay away long enough to lose OHIP and the travel policy can go with it. See the 212-day rule guide.
  • The cost of a cardiac event abroad. A US cardiac admission is among the most expensive claims there is, which is exactly why the questionnaire asks these questions so carefully. Our claim files page has real published figures.

Before you touch the questionnaire

  1. Find the exact date of any heart attack, stroke, TIA, bypass, angioplasty or stent. Not the year — the date.
  2. List every cardiac medication, including blood thinners, and the date each last changed.
  3. For each change, note why: a routine adjustment, or because something worsened? That word decides whether it counts.
  4. Note your blood pressure medication separately, and whether it altered in the last six months.
  5. Note anything unresolved — a stress test booked, results pending, a cardiology referral not yet seen.
  6. Count your total medications, for everything, not just your heart.
  7. Then compare. The premium is the same through an advisor or direct, so the advice costs nothing.

Frequently asked questions

Are blood thinners a heart condition on a travel insurance questionnaire?

A blood thinner is a medication, not a condition, and insurers differ on whether they ask about it directly. Some Canadian questionnaires name warfarin or Coumadin outright in their medication question. Others never mention anticoagulants, and the information reaches the insurer only through the question about your heart or circulatory condition. Either way the thing being assessed is the condition it was prescribed for. Declare both the condition and the medication, with the date each last changed.

My blood thinner is not warfarin. Do I still declare it?

Yes, and this is where honest answers go wrong. Questionnaires written years ago often name warfarin or Coumadin specifically, and someone on a newer anticoagulant reads that list, does not see their drug, and answers no in good faith. The question is almost always meant to capture any blood thinner. Declare it anyway and say so in writing.

Does a change in my blood thinner dose break my stability period?

Often not. Policy wording commonly carves out routine dose adjustments to blood thinners within prescribed parameters, where blood levels are checked regularly and the condition is unchanged, and some wording allows stopping one for up to 24 hours for a procedure. Anyone on warfarin has their dose tuned regularly, so this matters — but definitions differ, so confirm it in your own policy.

Which heart conditions make you ineligible for travel insurance?

Very few are refused by name. What makes a cardiac history difficult is how recently something happened, whether anything has changed since, and whether anything is unresolved. A stent placed eight years ago with nothing changed is routine; the same stent placed last month is not. A pending test, a symptom under investigation, a referral not attended or a recent admission will stop a sale outright at most insurers.

How do insurers ask about a heart attack, bypass or stent?

Usually as a recency band rather than a date — less than one year ago, one to five years, more than five, or none, choosing your most recent event. Which band you fall into moves the rate, so "about a year ago" can place you wrongly by a week. Find the actual date before you start.

Is high blood pressure treated as a heart condition?

It is usually asked on its own, separately, and often pivots on whether your medication was altered within the last six months. An alteration generally means the dose going up or down, a drug stopped, or a new one started — not a repeat prescription, and normally not a switch to an equivalent generic at the same dose. People on long-term blood pressure medication answer this wrong in both directions more than any other question.

Can I get travel insurance after a heart attack?

Commonly yes, and how long ago matters more than the fact of it. One several years back with stable treatment is routinely written. A recent one sits inside most stability windows and is a different conversation — which may still have an answer, because windows are each insurer's own wording and some insurers sell an add-on that buys a shorter one.

Why does an irregular heartbeat mean blood thinners, and does it change my travel insurance?

In atrial fibrillation the upper chambers quiver rather than beat cleanly, so blood pools — mostly in the left atrial appendage, where over 90% of AFib-related clots form — and a clot that breaks loose can travel to the brain. The anticoagulant is prescribed to prevent that stroke. For the questionnaire this matters more than people expect, because stroke is the single most-asked-about event on the form. A blood thinner taken for AFib is therefore treatment for a named heart condition, not an incidental pill. Declare the AFib, the anticoagulant, any rate or rhythm medication, and any stroke or TIA separately.

Does atrial fibrillation have to be declared?

Yes. AFib is named as a heart condition on several Canadian questionnaires and some ask about it separately from other arrhythmias. Declare the AFib, any blood thinner prescribed because of it, any rate or rhythm medication, and the date anything last changed. Years of stable AFib on unchanged medication is a far simpler case than AFib newly diagnosed or newly medicated.

I am a snowbird with a heart condition. What else should I watch?

Trip length, because an annual plan's per-trip cap is measured Canada to Canada and a long winter can leave most of the season uninsured. Returning home mid-winter, which can terminate the policy under some wordings. And provincial health coverage, because losing it can end the travel policy with it.

What happens if I answer the heart questions wrong?

The questionnaire sets your rate band, and insurers state that an inaccurate or incomplete answer can trigger an additional deductible or a declined claim. At 60 and over that deductible can run to tens of thousands of dollars, applied when the bill is largest. A cardiac claim abroad is among the most expensive there is, so this is the worst question set to guess on.
